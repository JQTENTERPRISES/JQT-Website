Why the last one failed

The last one was installed, paid for, and dead.

That is not a story about one bad vendor. It is what happens when a product is sold to the person who signs and shipped to the person who cleans. Two different people, two different jobs, and only one of them is ever in the room when the software gets chosen.

Who it is sold to
Buyer
Sees the dashboard. In a chair, on a laptop, both hands free, with somebody walking them through it. Signs it, renews it, and never cleans a room.
Who it is used by
Floor
One hand, their own phone, fourteen rooms and a cart. Nobody asked them. They vote by not opening it, and theirs is the vote that decides whether you got anything for the money.

Read the one star reviews and the complaint is rarely a missing feature. It is nearly always one of these four.

  • It is slower than paper. So the crew quietly goes back to pen and a group chat, and paper wins, because paper is the one thing everybody can share.
  • It logs you out mid shift. You are holding a bucket on the third floor. You are not going to your inbox for a code.
  • It eats your battery. It is your phone. You are not carrying a charger through fourteen rooms.
  • The photos land in your personal camera roll. A small thing, until you ask someone to photograph a stranger's bathroom.

The dead one in the headline is not a figure of speech. The property Kept was built in owned one, paid for it, and ran the floor on a radio and a printed list anyway.

The one number Kept is judged on

On the second room of the day, does the checklist get tapped step by step, standing in the room, or all at once at two in the afternoon, standing at the door?

In flow taps mean the times are real and you own a record. Batch taps mean Kept became the old app with better colors. That is the whole test, and you can run it yourself in an afternoon without asking us anything.

Which is why nobody gets trained. Day one is a sticky note with a first name and four digits, and one sentence from the manager: your rooms are on your phone today. That is the entire rollout. If Kept needs a class, it has already lost, because the next property's crew will not get one either.

The record

Who did what. Proven.

Every system in this category will hand you a report. A report is a claim, worth exactly as much as the vendor who printed it. Here is the difference between a report and a record, in three parts. You can check all three without taking our word for it, and the third one you can still check after we are gone.

01

The gate

Mark clean does not press until every step of the room checklist has been tapped. Not greyed out with an override for the manager. It does not press.

A room that is clean because someone said so from a chair at 4pm is the thing your current data is made of. This is the mechanic that ends it.

02

The chain

Every action appends to a log that cannot be quietly edited. Not by staff, not by the manager, not by us.

Each entry is built from the one before it, so altering an old line breaks every line after it, visibly, on a page your manager can open while you stand there.

03

The anchor

Once a day the head of that chain is sent to public RFC 3161 timestamp authorities, DigiCert and FreeTSA, and their signed reply is stored exactly as it came back.

The chain catches an edited row. The anchor catches the harder case: someone deletes the log and rebuilds a tidy fake whose hashes all recompute correctly. To fake history you would now also have to alter records held by a certificate authority.

And the proof outlives us. RFC 3161 is the same standard used to sign software and notarise documents. The reply is ordinary DER on your disk, so an auditor checks it years from now with openssl ts -verify, on a laptop, with no Kept account, no vendor, and no us. If we disappear, your record still stands up.

In South Florida that is not a nice touch, it is whether the crew can use the software at all.

The parts that break every other morning

Yesterday should not have to be re typed.

Most housekeeping tools are built around one clean day at a time. Real properties are not, and the gap between those two facts is where the manager's morning goes.

Out of order carries forward

With its reason attached. Nobody re marks the same four rooms every single morning, and no room quietly slips back into inventory because today's report did not know it was out.

Projects hold rooms across days

Recarpet 214. Repaint floor two. Ordered steps, a real deadline, many rooms at once, and a hold that survives every morning import until the work actually passes.

Supplies without a scanner

One tap, LOW or OUT. That is the floor's entire relationship with inventory, because a slower workflow is a dead workflow, and barcodes on a housekeeping cart are how you get neither the count nor the clean.

The buy list assembles itself, and a floor alert overrides the ledger, because the shelf is more honest than the spreadsheet.

A safety button that knows the room

It already knows where you are, because you started the room. Locating the person is the expensive half of every system in this category, and here it is a column that has been filling itself in all day.

The alert is never queued offline. If it cannot reach the server it says so, in your own language, and tells you to use the radio, because a queued panic alert is a lie told to someone in trouble. Raised, seen, and stood down each land on the chain, because nobody came and nobody was told are different facts.

Said plainly, because this is a legal area

Florida has no statewide employee safety device mandate, and several mandates elsewhere call for dedicated Bluetooth hardware, which a phone is not. So Kept does not describe this as ESD compliance, in the product or on this page, and it will not start. If you are in a city with a real ordinance, we will tell you what Kept does and does not satisfy before you buy it.

The honest part

Three things Kept does not do.

You will find these out anyway. Better on a web page you are reading for free than on the third call.

01

It does not replace your PMS

Kept reads the morning room status report you already run, and at the end of the shift produces a sheet that mirrors your PMS columns so your lead re keys the clean rooms in one pass.

It never writes into your PMS. That is a decision, not a gap: writing into a PMS means a certification cycle, and you would be waiting on that instead of running your floor.

02

It holds no guest data

Room numbers, staff first names, checklists, and photographs of broken things. No guest names, no reservations, no cards.

There is nothing in it worth stealing, which is the correct amount of guest data for a housekeeping tool to be carrying around on twelve personal phones.

03

It runs one property

Multi property is designed and it is not built. If you own eight hotels, the honest answer today is that Kept would run one of them properly while we build the rest of the answer in front of you.

Some owners want exactly that. Most do not. Assume this is the state of it until we tell you otherwise in writing.

Questions owners ask first

The things you would ask on the call anyway.

What if the morning report is late, missing, or malformed?

The day opens anyway, with every room dirty. A room wrongly listed dirty costs a walk through. A room wrongly listed clean costs a guest walking into an unmade bed. Kept picks the cheaper mistake on purpose, and the manager reconciles from the board rather than from a phone call.

What happens if our manager leaves?

The record does not leave with them. That is most of the point. Access is granted per person and revoked per person, a new manager is opened up from the back office rather than from a support queue, and the history the next manager inherits is the same history that was there the day before.
