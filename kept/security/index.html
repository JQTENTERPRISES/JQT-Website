Data and security

What is true today, and what is not built.

Security pages usually list everything the vendor wishes were true. This one lists what ships, then names what does not, because you can check either in about one question.

01

Your own instance

Kept runs one property per instance with its own database. Your operation is not a row in a table shared with other hotels, and there is no pooled dataset to be exposed by somebody else's mistake.

That is the trade we made first, ahead of the list below.

02

Access is per person

Every account is a person with a role: housekeeper, front desk, manager, owner. People see what their job requires. Access is granted and revoked per person from the back office rather than through a support queue.

Encrypted in transit with TLS. Backups run automatically on a schedule. Export or delete your data whenever you want.

03

There is little to steal

Room numbers, staff first names, checklists and photographs of broken things. No guest names, no reservations, no card data.

The blast radius of a housekeeping tool should be small, and this one's is.

Not built yet

Four things we will not claim.

These appeared on this site until September 2026 and they were not true. They are on the roadmap. They are not in the product.

01

Encryption at rest

The database is not encrypted on disk. Disk level encryption on the host is the current answer, which is not the same thing.

02

Single sign on

No SAML, no OAuth. Sign in is a first name and a PIN on the floor, a password in the office.

03

Two factor

Not implemented. A second factor on a shared phone in a linen closet also needs thought before it is worth the friction.

Point in time recovery is the fourth. Backups are scheduled snapshots, so recovery lands on a backup, not on an arbitrary minute. If any of these four is a requirement for you, say so on the call and we will tell you where it actually sits.

The part that is unusual

The record is verifiable without us.

Every action appends to a log nobody can quietly edit, including JQT, and its head is anchored daily to public RFC 3161 timestamp authorities. An auditor checks it years later with openssl and no account with anyone. How the record works.
